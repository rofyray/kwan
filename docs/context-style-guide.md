# Context style guide

How to write a "Try it in Ghana" practice setting.

When Kwan recommends a skill, it adds one short line that places the skill in everyday Ghanaian life. We call that line a **practice setting**. This guide is for teachers and contributors who write or review settings. Follow it and a child anywhere in Ghana should be able to read the setting and say, "I know this."

One rule sits above all the others: **if you are not sure something is true, leave it out.** A plain setting that is correct is better than a colourful one with a wrong price or a town in the wrong region.

## 1. What a practice setting is

A setting is shown after the words "Try it in Ghana". It can take one of two shapes.

- **A short story problem.** Someone in a real place does something with numbers, and the pupil answers one question.
- **A hands-on activity.** The pupil counts, measures, sorts, shares or asks classmates, using things found at home or at school. For example: *Ask ten classmates which food they like best: waakye, jollof, banku or fufu. Make a tally mark for each answer. Which food has the most marks?*

Every setting must:

1. practise **exactly the skill** of its indicator, with numbers inside what that indicator asks for (for example, `B4.1.1.1.2` works with numbers up to 100,000);
2. hold **one idea** and ask **one thing**;
3. be **true to Ghana**: real places, real money, prices and distances a child could meet;
4. be **short and easy to read** (see section 9);
5. be **written by you**, never copied from the NaCCA curriculum (see section 11).

Each indicator gets two or three settings. Kwan always shows the same one for the same skill, so write each setting to stand on its own.

- **Get the geography right.** Only four regions touch the sea: Western, Central, Greater Accra and Volta. A setting about sea fishing or a beach belongs there. Tamale, Kumasi and Sunyani have no beach.
- **Do not make one region "the village" and another "the city".** Every region has cities, towns and villages. Tamale and Kumasi are big cities. Greater Accra has farms and fishing communities.
- **A place name is not always needed.** "At the market", "on the school field" and "at the lorry station" work everywhere. When you do name a place, use a real one.

## 3. Names

Ghana has many peoples and many naming traditions. Across the settings you write:

- use names from **many groups**, not only Akan day names;
- use **girls and boys equally**, and give girls and boys the same kinds of things to do (see section 10);
- match the name to the place when the setting names a town, but do not overdo it: families from every group live in every big town;
- use first names only. Do not use the name of a real, known person.

The names below were checked for this guide. If a name is not here and you are not sure which group it belongs to, or whether it is a girl's or a boy's name, ask a reviewer from that group or pick a name from the table.

## 4. Money

### Coins and notes

Use only the coins and notes that exist today.

| | Values |
|---|---|
| Coins | 1 pesewa, 5 pesewas, 10 pesewas, 20 pesewas, 50 pesewas, GH₵ 1, GH₵ 2 |
| Notes | GH₵ 1, GH₵ 2, GH₵ 5, GH₵ 10, GH₵ 20, GH₵ 50, GH₵ 100, GH₵ 200 |

- 100 pesewas make GH₵ 1.
- GH₵ 1 and GH₵ 2 exist both as a coin and as a note. Say which one you mean if it matters.
- There is no 25 pesewas coin, no GH₵ 3 note, no GH₵ 25 note and no GH₵ 500 note.
- Small coins are real money, but very little at the market is priced below 50 pesewas. Use the 1, 5, 10 and 20 pesewas coins for counting coins and making change, not as the price of a meal.
- Do not use old cedi amounts from before 2007, even though some people still say them.

### How to write money

| Write | Do not write |
|---|---|
| `GH₵ 7.50` | `GHS 7.50`, `GHC7.50`, `Ghc 7.5`, `¢7.50`, `7.50 cedis`, `$7.50` |
| `GH₵ 12.00` or `GH₵ 12` | `GH₵12` (no space), `GH₵ 12.0` |
| `GH₵ 1,250.00` | `GH₵ 1250.00`, `GH₵ 1.250,00` |
| `50 pesewas` | `50p`, `GH₵ .50`, `50 Gp` |
| a `GH₵ 20` note, a `GH₵ 2` coin, a `50 pesewas` coin | a `20 cedis bill`, a `2 cedi piece` |

The pattern is: `GH₵`, one space, the cedis, then a point and two digits when there are pesewas. Whole amounts may drop the pesewas (`GH₵ 5`). Inside one setting, write every amount the same way.

## 5. Realistic prices

A price that is far from real life breaks the setting. A child knows that a ball of kenkey does not cost 5 pesewas and that a trotro fare is not GH₵ 500.

Prices also rise from year to year and differ from town to town, so this guide does not fix exact prices. Use the bands below as a rough guide, then **check each price against what you paid this term in your own area**. The reviewer does the same.

| Thing | Rough band |
|---|---|
| One orange, one sachet of water, one pencil | 50 pesewas to a few cedis |
| A ball of kenkey, a small loaf, an exercise book | A few cedis to about GH₵ 10 |
| A short trotro ride in town | A few cedis to about GH₵ 10 |
| A plate of waakye or jollof | About GH₵ 10 to a few tens of cedis |
| A school bag or a pair of school shoes | Tens of cedis to a few hundred cedis |
| A big bag of rice or maize | Several hundred cedis |

More rules for prices and amounts:

- Use prices that end in `.00` or `.50` for market goods. Keep amounts like `GH₵ 3.47` for upper primary decimal work, and then say why the amount is odd (a bill shared between friends, for example).
- The change must be possible with real coins and notes.
- Keep quantities sensible: a child carries 2 loaves, not 200; a trotro holds about 15 passengers, not 150; a class has tens of pupils, not thousands.
- Large numbers need a setting that really has large numbers: the people at a stadium, the kilograms of cocoa a district sells, the population of a town. Do not invent an exact figure for a real place. Say "about" or use a made-up farm, school or shop.

Real ways things are sold at the market are useful for maths:

- kenkey by the **ball**, yam by the **tuber**, plantain by the **bunch** or the **finger**;
- tomatoes, pepper and garden eggs by the **heap**;
- gari, rice, beans and maize by the **olonka** (a measuring tin) or the **bowl**;
- eggs by the **crate** (30 eggs);
- cocoa beans by the **bag** (64 kg).

## 6. Realistic distances, measures and time

- Use **metric units**: kilometres, metres, centimetres, kilograms, grams, litres, millilitres. No miles, feet, pounds, ounces or gallons. Temperature is in degrees Celsius.
- A child walks metres or a few kilometres. A trotro covers a few kilometres in town or tens of kilometres between towns. A journey between regions is hundreds of kilometres and takes hours.
- For a journey between two named towns, use the real distance, rounded, and say "about". Check a map. These road distances are safe to use:

| Journey | By road |
|---|---|
| Accra to Tema | about 30 km |
| Bolgatanga to Navrongo | about 30 km |
| Accra to Cape Coast | about 145 km |
| Tamale to Bolgatanga | about 160 km |
| Accra to Kumasi | about 250 km |
| Kumasi to Tamale | about 380 km |

- Ghana has a **rainy season** and a **dry season**, and the dry, dusty **harmattan** wind around the turn of the year. There is no snow, no winter and no autumn.
- Ghana does not change its clocks during the year. Write clock times as `9:15 am` and `2:45 pm`, or in 24-hour form when the indicator asks for it.
- School language: classes are **Basic 1 to Basic 6**, the year has **three terms**, and pupils use exercise books, pencils, chalk, rulers and desks.

## 7. Everyday life to draw on

Pick settings from the whole of daily life, in every part of the country.

**Food.** Kenkey, banku, fufu, waakye, jollof rice, tuo zaafi, rice balls, gari, red red, kelewele, koko, koose, bofrot, roasted plantain, groundnuts, fried fish, tilapia, shito, groundnut soup, light soup, palm nut soup, okro stew, kontomire stew. Fruits and crops: oranges, mangoes, pineapples, pawpaw, bananas, coconuts, watermelon, sugarcane, plantain, cassava, yam, cocoyam, maize, millet, rice, beans, tomatoes, pepper, onions, garden eggs, okro. Drinks: water, sobolo, coconut water.

**Markets and trade.** A stall, a tray carried on the head, a kiosk, a provisions shop, a tailor or seamstress, a hairdresser, a carpenter, a mason, a fitter, a mobile money agent (do not name the company).

**Farming.** Cocoa farms in the forest regions (Western North, Ashanti, Ahafo, Eastern, Central and Western among them). Shea nuts, millet, groundnuts and yams in the northern regions. Maize, cassava, plantain, rice, vegetables, oil palm, cashew, pineapples and mangoes. Goats, sheep, cattle, chickens and guinea fowl. School gardens.

**Fishing.** Canoes on the sea in the four coastal regions, with landing beaches in towns such as Elmina, Axim, Keta and Ada Foah, and the fishing harbour at Tema. Canoes on Lake Volta, in towns such as Yeji, Kpando, Kete Krachi and Dambai. Fish is smoked, fried, salted and sold by the basin or the piece.

**Transport.** Trotro (the fare is paid to the mate), shared taxi, taxi, okada (motorbike taxi), motor tricycle, long-distance bus, bicycle, canoe, the ferry across Lake Volta, walking. Write "lorry station" or "trotro station" for the place where vehicles load.

**Games and play.** Ampe (a jumping and clapping game where players score points), oware (a board with 12 houses and 4 seeds in each house at the start, so 48 seeds), chaskele (a bat and ball game played with a stick and a crushed tin), pilolo, ludo, draughts, skipping and football.

**Craft and culture.** Kente strips and patterns, adinkra symbols, smocks, beads, baskets, clay pots, drumming and dancing, festivals and durbars. Patterns in cloth and beads are very good for the Algebra strand. Treat every culture with respect and do not explain a custom unless you know it well.

**School and home.** Break time, assembly, school feeding, the school garden, sweeping the compound, fetching water, reading under a tree, a church or mosque as a landmark, the borehole, the chop bar, the lorry park.

## 8. No brand names

Do not name any company, product, shop chain, bank, phone network, transport company, app or sports club. Write what the thing is.

| Do not write | Write |
|---|---|
| a named chocolate drink | a tin of chocolate drink |
| a named soft drink | a bottle of soft drink |
| a named mobile money service | mobile money |
| a named bus company | a long-distance bus |
| a named supermarket | a shop, a supermarket |

Public places are fine: a named market, a town, a lake, a national park. "Pure water" is the everyday word for a sachet of drinking water and is not a brand.

## 9. Length and reading level

- **220 characters at most**, counting spaces and punctuation. Shorter is better.
- **Basic 1 to Basic 3:** very short sentences and everyday words. The automatic check asks for a Flesch-Kincaid grade of 5 or lower.
- **Basic 4 to Basic 6:** still short and plain. The check asks for a Flesch-Kincaid grade of 7 or lower.

The Flesch-Kincaid grade goes up when sentences are long and when words have many syllables. To keep it low:

- write one fact per sentence;
- prefer "buys", "shares", "has", "gives" to "purchases", "distributes", "possesses", "donates";
- put the question in its own sentence, last;
- use digits for numbers (`8 oranges`), and write maths words the way the curriculum teaches them at that level;
- keep local words in their usual spelling, with no italics and no explanation: trotro, okada, kenkey, waakye, kelewele, ampe, oware, chaskele, kente, adinkra.

Use British spelling, as Ghanaian schools do: metre, litre, colour, favourite, organise.

## 10. No stereotypes, and keep children safe

**People.**

- Girls and boys do the same things. Girls play football, repair bicycles, fish with their fathers and come first in maths. Boys cook, sell at the market, fetch water and look after younger children.
- Do not attach a character trait, a job or a level of wealth to any ethnic group, religion or region.
- Do not link any region or any group with poverty, and do not link any other with being rich or modern.
- Show many kinds of families: parents, a grandmother, an auntie or uncle, older brothers and sisters.
- Include children with disabilities as ordinary pupils doing ordinary things, without making the disability the point of the story.
- No job is a joke and no job is a punishment. A trader, a farmer, a fisher, a nurse, a driver, a mate, a teacher and an engineer all deserve respect.

**Children are pupils first.** A child may help at a stall or on the farm after school or in the holidays, as many children do. Never show a child working instead of going to school, and never treat that as normal.

**Leave these out completely:** alcohol, tobacco and drugs; betting, lotteries and gambling for money; weapons, fighting and crime; illness, accidents and death; illegal mining; party politics; chieftaincy and land disputes; arguments about religion; insults, body size and skin colour; anything frightening. If you have to ask whether a topic is suitable for a seven-year-old, it is not.

## 11. Never copy NaCCA exemplar sentences

The NaCCA curriculum gives exemplars under each indicator. They belong to NaCCA and the Ministry of Education. Kwan's settings must be Kwan-written, so that they can be shared under an open licence.

- Read the indicator and its exemplar to understand the **maths** the pupil should do.
- Then close the PDF and write a new setting in your own words, with your own place, people, objects and numbers.
- Do not paste an exemplar and change a few words. Do not keep its sentence order with new names. A reviewer who sees a setting that looks like the exemplar will reject it.
- Ideas are free to use. If the exemplar shares oranges, you may share groundnuts, or measure cloth, or count passengers.

The same rule covers textbooks, past questions and websites: do not copy from them.

## 12. Six good examples

Each is under 220 characters, uses real places and real money, and asks one clear question.

> **Good 1 (Basic 1).** Akua has 8 oranges on her tray at Makola Market. She sells 3. How many oranges are left on the tray?

Why it works: a real market, small numbers, three short sentences, one question.

> **Good 2 (Basic 2).** Yao has one GH₵ 2 coin and three 50 pesewas coins. How much money does he have altogether?

Why it works: only coins that exist, money written the right way, and a sum a child can check with real coins.

> **Good 3 (Basic 3).** An oware board has 12 houses. Tetteh puts 4 seeds in each house to start a game. How many seeds does he use?

Why it works: a game pupils know, with the real numbers of the game, so the multiplication is true to life.

> **Good 4 (Basic 4).** Esinam and her father catch 36 tilapia on the lake at Kpando. They put 9 fish in each basin. How many basins do they fill?

Why it works: Kpando really is on Lake Volta, a girl is out fishing, and the division comes out exactly.

> **Good 5 (Basic 5).** Fuseini buys 3 exercise books at GH₵ 4.50 each in Tamale. He pays with a GH₵ 20 note. How much change does he get?

Why it works: a believable price, a real note, two steps, and the change can be given in real coins and notes.

> **Good 6 (Basic 6).** The road from Tamale to Bolgatanga is about 160 km. Lamisi's bus has covered three quarters of the way. How many kilometres are left?

Why it works: a real journey with its real distance, the word "about", and a fraction that gives a whole number.

## 13. Six bad examples

> **Bad 1.** Tom has 5 dollars. He buys a hot dog for 2 dollars at the baseball game. How much money is left?

What is wrong: foreign money, foreign food and a foreign sport. The pupil has to decode the story before starting the maths. This is the problem Kwan exists to fix.

> **Bad 2.** Kofi pays GHC3.5 for bread with a GH₵ 3 note and a 25 pesewas coin.

What is wrong: there is no GH₵ 3 note and no 25 pesewas coin, the amount should be written `GH₵ 3.50`, and there is no question to answer.

> **Bad 3.** Ama buys a ball of kenkey for 5 pesewas, then walks from the beach at Tamale to Accra before lunch.

What is wrong: the price is far too low, Tamale is far from the sea, and Tamale to Accra is hundreds of kilometres. Three things a child knows are false.

> **Bad 4.** Kwame buys 4 tins of Milo at the supermarket. Each tin costs GH₵ 60. How much does he pay?

What is wrong: a brand name. Write "4 tins of chocolate drink".

> **Bad 5.** Abena stays at home to cook and sweep while her brother Kofi goes to school. She sweeps 3 rooms a day. How many rooms does she sweep in 5 days?

What is wrong: it shows a girl kept out of school to do housework and treats it as normal. It is a stereotype and it is unsafe.

> **Bad 6.** During the annual harvest celebration, which is traditionally attended by numerous dignitaries, a prosperous agricultural entrepreneur distributes approximately 1,250 kilograms of produce proportionally among several neighbouring communities, so calculate each community's allocation.

What is wrong: it is longer than 220 characters, it is one long sentence full of hard words, and "several" gives the pupil no number to work with.

## 14. Checklist before you submit

The automatic checks (`kwan context check`) look for these on every setting:

- [ ] One tidy line: starts with a capital letter or a digit, ends with a full stop or a question mark, single spaces.
- [ ] 220 characters or fewer.
- [ ] No em dash.
- [ ] Reading level within the limit for the class (section 9). Names from the term list, other words with a capital letter inside a sentence, and numbers do not count against you, so "Atampoka in Bolgatanga" costs nothing. A made-up name at the very start of a sentence is read as an ordinary word, so use names from the term list or from section 3.
- [ ] Money written in the `GH₵ 7.50` pattern, and only real coins and notes when a coin or note is named (`a GH₵ 3 note` fails).
- [ ] At least one Ghanaian term from Kwan's list (`src/kwan/context/ghana_terms.txt`): a place, a name, a food, a game, transport, or the cedi.
- [ ] No word from the banned list (`src/kwan/context/banned_terms.txt`: brands, unsafe topics, stereotypes, foreign money and units).
- [ ] Not a near copy of another setting of the same indicator.

A person has to check the rest:

- [ ] The setting practises exactly the indicator's skill, and the answer is correct.
- [ ] Every place is real and in the right region. Every coin and note exists.
- [ ] Prices, quantities and distances are believable this year.
- [ ] Names come from many groups, with girls and boys equally across your settings.
- [ ] No brand names, no stereotypes, nothing unsafe for a young child.
- [ ] Not copied from the NaCCA curriculum, a textbook or a website.
- [ ] If you were not sure a detail was true, you left it out.

Settings are shipped only after a teacher has reviewed them. The [teacher review guide](teacher-review-guide.md) explains how.
